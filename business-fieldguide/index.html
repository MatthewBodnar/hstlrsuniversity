01

The foundations  ·  2 min read

Speed. Nothing can be slow.

Everything that needs to be done must be done fast.

That doesn't mean it has to be done cheap or shit. It means it has to be done quickly. People seem to conflate the two. That if you do something quickly, it must be terrible. That's not the case.

You learn to punch. The instructor says, faster, faster, faster. You don't suddenly forget how to punch. You keep the same quality of punch, but you do it quicker. Apply that to your work.

You need a website. Somebody says two weeks. Two weeks for what? The smallest version that does the job, or a fortnight of sitting around? Everyone thinks things take forever. Things don't take forever. Things can be done quickly.

It's been a week. Where's your business?

“I have a business idea.”

A week later: “How's your business?”

“What business?”

You have an idea and it's been a week. Where's your fucking company?

Get the advert out. Call the buyer. Put the offer in front of somebody. A fast first attempt gives you something to improve while the slow guy is still deciding what font to use.

The faster you produce an advert, the faster it's out, the faster you get results. If you do it quick enough, you might be able to produce two adverts in the time that other people take to produce one.

Move. Find out. Fix it. Move again. A business needs forward momentum. Your idea sitting in your head has none.

Get this in your head.

Speed. Nothing can be slow.

02

The foundations  ·  2 min read

Money in. Not playing business.

Money in is a business. Money out is vanity.

You say you want to start a company. Then comes the shopping list. An office. A logo. Labels. Laptops. Stock. A warehouse. Spend, spend, spend, spend.

You're talking about spending money. I'm talking about making money.

We need an office. Why do you need an office? No one's paid you yet. And you're talking about an office? I need a new laptop. Why? No one's paid you yet.

It's not a business if no one pays you. Your beautiful setup proves you know how to buy things. It hasn't proved you can sell a damn thing.

Make the first bet small.

Take the makeup business. Build the whole operation first: £200,000. Test a smaller offer first: let's say £5,000. That's £195,000 you haven't put on the line.

So let's assume both companies are bound to fail. Here you've lost five grand. Here you've lost 200 grand. Why risk all that money when you can do it cheap?

Show the product. Make the price clear. Know how you'll deliver. If it's a pre-order, say it's a pre-order. Find out if anybody actually wants it before you fill your bedroom with 500 boxes.

You have skills, a phone, a laptop, people you can work with. Use what you've got. Keep your job while the first sales come in. Quitting doesn't magically turn a bad idea into a business.

When you want to start a business, the first question is, where's the money? How are you going to get paid?

Get this in your head.

Where’s the money in?

03

The foundations  ·  2 min read

A million in sales. Nothing left.

You can have a million-pound business and still be fucking broke.

Three deals at £40,000. That's £120,000 brought in. At 10% commission, £12,000 for the salesman. Beautiful. Now look underneath the big number.

A television advertising agency sells complete packages for £18,000–£19,000. Production. Approvals. About 150 adverts on television. Clients everywhere. Fancy office. New cars.

On some packages, only £2,000–£3,000 is left before overhead. On others, almost nothing. The first year turns over nearly £1 million.

But there was no profit.

The next deal pays for the previous deal. Then you need another deal. And another. Stop selling for a month and the entire thing blows up.

You haven't built a cash machine. You've built a machine that needs feeding. Speed can keep it moving for a while. It can't make a losing deal profitable.

Stop being impressed by the wrong number.

Revenue is what you sell. Profit is what survives the costs. Cash is what can actually pay the bill when it lands. Know all three.

+$100k

$1 million in revenue at 20% margin: $200,000. At 30%: $300,000. Same sales. Fifty percent more profit. That's why the margin matters.

Don't tell me how many clients you've got, how many staff you've hired, or how busy you are. What's left? If you can't answer that, get the numbers in front of you.

Get this in your head.

Money coming through is not the same as money you keep.

04

The foundations  ·  2 min read

Why would they give you their money?

You make money because people decide to give it to you.

Forget the vague phrase “making money” for a second. Somebody has money. You want them to give some of it to you. Why are they going to give you their money? Why?

That's a serious question. Get a piece of paper and write down. I have this. I'm selling to this person. They will give me their money because.

And you should be able to name a bunch of reasons. And if you can't, you have a problem.

Money's like water and water is always moving.

It moves through buyers, suppliers, services, shops. And if you stand in the right place at the right time, you're going to get wet. Get useful where money is already moving.

You don't have to make the thing.

A buyer wants a product. Somebody else can supply it. You're in the middle, finding the buyer, arranging the job, making sure it gets done. That's a business too.

Take a record label. Find artists. Agree a split. Get their music distributed. A £1 sale, split 50/50, gives each side 50p before costs. Build a catalog. Put the work out. You're selling distribution and effort, not an expensive office.

The product doesn't need to be glamorous. Concrete. Cleaning. Makeup. Whatever. Your passion isn't a payment method. Demand, a reason to choose you, and delivery. That's the mechanism.

Know your buyer. A struggling company wants sales. An established company might want visibility or status. Same advertising package, different reason to buy. Find the reason.

Get this in your head.

I have this. I’m selling to this person. They will give me their money because…

05

The customer  ·  2 min read

Nobody cares about your massage oil.

You have to learn to view all of your offers from your buyer’s eyes.

You run a massage business. You charge more because you use the best oil. You're a massage-oil expert. Wonderful.

Does he know anything about massage oil? Does he even give a fuck? Has he tried bad massage oil? Does he know the difference?

To them, oil is oil.

Your competitor is $20 cheaper. As far as the customer can see, it's the same appointment. Your brilliant technical detail hasn't become a reason to choose you.

You need to educate the buyer why that matters. Otherwise, they don't give a fuck.

Sell what it does for them.

Don't sell beans in a cup. “Tired? Nice warm coffee.” There's a reason to walk through the door.

A pen isn't just plastic and blue ink. It's somewhere to put an idea. A website isn't a page count. It's a way for customers to find, trust, and pay a business. Sell the result they want, then show how you deliver it.

And give them a reason to care now. A maybe-useful benefit two years away won't automatically solve today's objection.

Ask somebody who'll call your bullshit.

Ask your mother about your company.

“That's expensive.” “What difference does the oil make?” “How do I book?” Good. You're finally hearing the gaps. Make the benefit clear. Make booking easy. Then put it in front of the actual buyers.

Do not view yourself from your own knowledgeable eyes. Stop selling the detail you're obsessed with. Sell the difference they care about.

Get this in your head.

Sell the need. Sell the result.

06

The customer  ·  2 min read

Never sell on price.

You do not want to compete on price.

There's a phone case getting shipped across the world for $0.89. The case and delivery. How the hell are you going to beat that?

You need to sell on brand. Here in the West, living where we live, that's our advantage. We have the whole idea of branding and quality and reputation. These are the things you need to sell on.

“I want to make it really affordable.” Fine. Will the price pay for the work, the selling, the support, and you? Or have you just made yourself the busiest broke person in the room?

Give the higher price a reason.

You need to build your reputation, build your credibility, build on the point that you're an expert in your field. And as you do that, your price will increase.

A $300 website with $100 of delivery leaves $200 before your other costs. Charge $150 for the same job and only $50 remains. Twice the price. Four times the contribution.

But a higher price still needs a buyer. Show the better work. Be reliable. Solve a sharper problem. Make the difference visible instead of just sticking a bigger number on the same weak pitch.

There's only a certain amount of customers in the world.

If you can reach 100 buyers, getting more value from the right offer to those buyers can be easier than finding another 100. You don't need everybody. You need buyers who want what you do.

Get this in your head.

Build your reputation. Build your credibility.

07

The customer  ·  2 min read

Get attention. Then sell something.

Attention is the currency of the modern world.

All advertising is, is buying attention, which is fine. But buying attention is more expensive than getting attention for free.

Free financially. It still takes time, effort, or creativity. That's your job. Get noticed.

A vehicle-wrapping shop paints a $3,000 mural on the wall. People drive past, see the work, and want their cars wrapped there. He got attention. He fucking painted his wall.

If you have to go and run a marathon dressed as a fucking dinosaur with your company name on the back, then go do it. It's effectively free and it's good for you.

You're getting views. Where's the sale?

What most people do is, you see a lot of people, they find ways to get attention, but they don't monetize it.

Don't lose the point of the whole thing. Get attention and then do not be afraid to shamelessly sell, sell, sell. The offer needs to be right there.

Curiosity helps. Why is this different? What's behind that result? Give somebody a reason to look closer. Then make the answer worth looking for.

Remember a returning customer's name. Make the setting memorable. Publish another useful piece of work. Visibility doesn't always require a circus.

And before you spend your afternoon arguing online, ask what you're trying to get. A customer? Relevant attention? Or just the pleasure of being a loud idiot?

Attention is an opening. You've still got to do something with it.

Get this in your head.

Get attention. Give it somewhere to go.

08

The customer  ·  2 min read

It ain’t real till they pay.

Until the money’s in the bank, it ain’t real.

£600,000 campaign. £250,000 expected profit. The meeting goes beautifully. They love the plan. They sign the contract. You're already spending the money in your head.

Then the marketing boss gets sick. The junior contact can't authorize the payment. The big boss won't proceed. The invoice sits there.

And guess what? It never comes.

Read that number on the right again. Zero. That's what you can actually spend. Keep the contract. Keep following up. But stop running your bank balance on excitement.

Speak like you believe your own offer.

Being good at speaking will make you be good at selling.

You've got a good product and a slick presentation, but you're mumbling through it like you're apologizing for existing. Practice. Slow down. Breathe. Put some energy behind what you're saying.

You don't have to be that way. There's other ways you can also be engaging without being the same as me.

Bring proof. Show actual work. Let people see that real customers buy from you. Do not be a bullshitter, but you need to be super self-confident.

Ask for the decision.

Ten maybes aren't ten sales. Five yeses and five noes at least tell you where you stand. Explain the offer, the real deadline, the next step. Ask. Accept the answer. Move.

You can spend all week feeling close to a deal. The bank doesn't care how close you felt.

Get this in your head.

A maybe does not pay your bills.

09

The customer  ·  2 min read

Resell to people you’ve already sold to.

It is so much easier to sell to someone who’s already bought from you than it is to sell to someone new.

You're hunting for strangers while ignoring the people who already gave you money. Why? They know you. They've tried the product. If you delivered, you've already crossed the hardest bridge.

Take the advertising package. £18,000–£19,000 upfront. Make the advert. Show it to the customer. Let them love it. Then offer £5,000 of extra airtime.

The add-on left roughly £4,000 in that agency example. Around 60–70% took it when they liked their advert. Pitching £24,000–£25,000 cold was a much harder sell.

First, give them something they're happy they bought. Then offer the next useful thing.

The money might be sitting in your database.

A business owner wants to borrow £200,000. He's got 10,000 past customers. Before you chase a loan, what can you offer those people? A repeat purchase? An upgrade? A proper returning-customer deal?

In business, you don't need to sell to tons of people if you can sell to the same people over and over again.

Trust is worth more than a clever trick.

Refund when a refund is deserved. Follow up personally. Make a real bundle worth buying. You don't need an invented story about a product update to offer somebody something better.

A $17 purchase, refunded, followed by a $40 bundle is $40 total revenue. Not $57. The order value is up about 135%. Great—now count the costs and the buyers who actually take it.

Get this in your head.

Resell to people you’ve already sold to.

10

People & systems  ·  2 min read

Need a partner? Or just somebody to talk to?

Make sure you need your partners and they need you.

People want partners because they like the idea of having someone to vibe off of or work with or someone who they can check their thinking with. That's fine.

But “I don't want to do it alone” isn't automatically a reason to give away half the company. Do you need a cofounder? A specialist? A reliable second-in-command? Know which problem you're solving.

If you have a partner and you both need each other and it's 50-50, good. I'm not saying don't have partners.

You can sell. They can build. You can find customers. They can deliver. Now you have a reason to work together. Sort out responsibility, money, decisions, and what happens if somebody leaves.

Stop repeating “never work with family.”

A trusted brother, cousin, or friend might have exactly the skill you're missing. Pool resources. Stop buying two of everything just to feel independent. But agree the deal properly. Closeness doesn't do the work for you.

Same with suppliers. A good supplier wants repeat orders. You want good work and a fair price. Build something both sides want to keep. Clear terms and sensible payment timing matter more than hoping you'll enjoy chasing each other later.

And when you meet somebody who knows more—listen.

You already know your own business. You already know how you do things.

Stop using every conversation to perform how clever you are. Ask what they know. Bring useful people useful things. Introductions. Work. Information. A network is people who can actually rely on each other.

Get this in your head.

Mutual self-interest. Make the relationship work for both sides.

11

People & systems  ·  2 min read

An assistant to do what, exactly?

Staff either make you money or save you time.

“I need an assistant.”

An assistant to do what? Oh, just to help me with things. What things? What things will they help with? How long do those things take? How much time is that going to save you?

There it is. An actual job. Or a vague wish to feel like somebody's boss.

A service studio reaches 72 performers and around 600,000 in one month's turnover. Big rents. Multiple locations. Management everywhere. Later, the strongest ten make the smaller operation easier to run and more profitable.

Big headcount. Big turnover. Big fucking headache. Look at what the operation actually keeps.

Put the right person in the right job.

A knight doesn't move like a bishop. Your brilliant designer might be a terrible cold caller. Fine. Let them design. Pay for skills that remove your weak spots. Measure the work that matters.

Some people bring sales. Others save hours, prevent mistakes, or make delivery work. Know what you're paying for. Busy is not the same as useful.

Loyalty goes both ways.

I'm loyal to everyone who works for me. I will always make sure they get paid. If I have a thousand in my bank and I owe them a thousand, I'll go without money. I do my part. They do their part. Loyalty's both ways.

Pay them. Give them direction. Recognize good work. Build something they want to help win. You don't buy loyalty by calling people “family” while you break your promises.

Get this in your head.

Staff need a clear objective.

12

People & systems  ·  2 min read

Don’t make anyone irreplaceable.

Never make anybody irreplaceable.

You're a guy, you're busy, you get an assistant. Your assistant is exceptionally good. Your assistant knows all the passwords and shit. You don't know because you're busy and you're in a rush. You know the end of the story.

That isn't delegation. That's handing somebody the only set of keys.

Or it's the star salesman. Better than everybody else. He leaves. Suddenly nobody can sell properly.

Here's what you do before that happens. “You're really good. Every Friday morning, teach the others the pitch. Go through the script. Show them what you're saying on the phone.”

Reward it. Friday afternoon off. Extra money. A proper benefit. You're paying to turn one person's skill into a stronger team.

If you see or identify someone as irreplaceable or you look at your company and go, I couldn't survive without this person, then you're in trouble.

Your absence shouldn't switch everything off.

Four prospectors. Two founders. Good energy every morning. Then the founders disappear for ten days and the staff leave. A room full of enthusiasm wasn't a system.

Make the goals clear. Share the method. Keep access recoverable. Let people handle the small decisions without waiting for you to wake up.

And lead by example. If you demand follow-through, follow through. If you expect effort, show effort. Build a team that can move. Not an audience waiting for your next instruction.

Get this in your head.

If you couldn’t survive without one person, you’ve got work to do.

13

People & systems  ·  2 min read

You want an office. You don’t need one.

Do I need to spend this money right now?

You're working from home. Boxes around the bed. Laptop on the desk. Orders coming in. Now you've made some money and you want an office.

No, you want an office. You don't need an office.

You're making money without an office. Setting up an office, is that going to bring in more orders?

Where's the actual problem? Not enough customers? Spend on reaching them. Orders backing up? Improve delivery. Errors everywhere? Fix the process. Spend against the problem you've got.

The €15,000 backend.

Automatic membership access. Purchase tracking. Accounts linked up. Very clever. The developer wants €15,000. But the team is already doing the job manually, and it's working.

“How does this bring more people through the front door?”

No answer? Then it can wait. When manual delivery becomes the bottleneck, revisit it. Buy capacity when capacity is the problem. Don't buy complexity because somebody can make it sound impressive.

The camera nobody asked for.

Your current camera delivers the 4K footage the customer needs. A new camera costs 20,000. What changes for the customer? Better work they value, or a new toy for you?

I'm not saying don't invest. I'm not saying be stingy. I'm not saying any of that. I'm saying be careful and think, do I need to spend this money right now?

Sometimes refusing to invest will kill you too. The market moves. Your tools break. Think. Then spend. Don't just go shopping because the bank balance finally looks nice.

Get this in your head.

If you spend money, know exactly what it changes.

14

Staying power  ·  2 min read

The business is supposed to pay you.

The point of a business is not to have a business for vanity.

You're turning money over, rescuing the company, buying another computer, hiring another person. Everybody gets paid. Except you.

I kept the company alive at the expense of me.

That's the agency mistake. Every fresh deal gets poured back into keeping a weak model alive. It finally collapses and the owner has very little to show for the years of work.

Don't build a bigger business and a smaller life.

Your balance isn't all your money.

Some of it belongs to delivery. Some to wages. Some to bills that haven't landed yet. Money in the bank can already have a job. Know what's committed before you start spending it like profit.

Then make room for actual owner income and a reserve. You need options. A business model that works today can get undercut tomorrow. Betting everything on it lasting forever is a stupid bet.

Cash timing can strangle you.

You pay for the work now. The big customer wants 90 days to pay you. The job can look profitable on paper while your cash runs dry in the middle.

Know when the money comes in. Know when it goes out. Agree payment stages. Chase what you're owed. Deal with bills before they become emergencies.

You don't fix bad economics by taking another deposit and hoping. And you don't become successful by spending money that's needed to keep a promise. Get paid properly. Keep what you earn. Stop confusing survival with success.

Get this in your head.

Keep the company healthy. Make sure it’s actually paying you.

15

Staying power  ·  2 min read

The flat tyre won’t fix itself.

If you have a problem inside of your company or a problem making money, you better do something about it quickly.

Your car has a flat tyre. You leave it for three months. Come back. Has it fixed itself?

Of course it fucking hasn't. So why do you treat your business like that?

“It's been a slow couple of months.”

“What have you been doing?”

“We're thinking about placing some adverts.”

Months. And now you're thinking about it.

If you've got a problem inside of your business and you've identified it, you need to start thinking of a battle plan to fix it quickly.

Care about it. Keep your head.

The website breaks. Payments get stuck. A customer is angry. Panic doesn't solve any of it. Neither does shrugging.

Find the failure. Give the fix an owner. Follow up. If you need a manual fallback, keep payments and orders matched. Don't create ten more broken promises while fixing the first one.

And answer the customer. Let them tell you what's wrong. Tell them what happens next. Do it. Your reputation gets built when the easy part is over.

Change something small. Watch what happens.

A few quiet days led to roughly $20 of changes to a service's presentation, including a $10 costume. Sales improved. Maybe the changes helped. Maybe the quiet spell was already ending.

Don't pretend you know which one without checking. Make small changes you can reverse. Look at the result. Keep the improvement. Stop sitting there hoping somebody else will care enough to fix your problem.

Get this in your head.

What can you do about it now?

16

Staying power  ·  2 min read

Make it easy to give you money.

You need to create a golden bridge for people to pay you money.

Somebody wants to buy. Your checkout doesn't work for them. They ask for a bank transfer. You make it difficult.

You've done the hard part. You've found a buyer. Now you're arguing with the payment.

It has to be very, very easy to give you money.

An event sponsor doesn't want to pay cash. He offers roof tiles. $5,000 worth. The organizer knows someone who wants tiles, sells them for $4,500, and keeps the sponsor.

Bang, you may lose 500 bucks. Bang, bang, bang, bang. Transaction done. Doesn't lose a sponsor.

That's resourcefulness. The stock had a buyer. The numbers worked. Don't accept a mountain of junk you can't use or sell and call yourself clever.

And don't leave yourself one switch away from dead.

One payment provider. One bank. One account with your entire audience. One assistant with every password.

Any one of them goes down and you're sitting there, unable to trade. That's a stupid amount of dependence.

Have another payment route ready. Keep customer contact recoverable. Know how to restore the site. Use more than one distribution channel. Make the backup real before you need it.

It doesn't matter how fast you can sell if a single blocked account stops every buyer from paying you.

Get this in your head.

Make it very, very easy for people to pay you.

17

Staying power  ·  2 min read

Chaos is opportunity.

Every time stuff’s fucked up, every time there’s chaos, every time there’s a mess, there is an opportunity.

Everybody is staring at what stopped working. Look at what people suddenly need.

Creators lose their advertising revenue. They've still got viewers. They need another way to earn. They want merchandise, but they haven't set it up.

Find a good merchandise supplier. Bring ten creators. Negotiate a cut of the turnover you introduce. Let's say 5%.

Now, every time Jackass1 and Jackass2 sells a hat, you get paid. Before, you didn't get none of their money. You just knew them.

Same people. Same audience. A new problem. You put the pieces together.

Your old business doesn't get a lifetime pass.

A television advertising pitch that worked years ago faces a new kind of competition when a buyer can pay a creator, reach the audience, and see the response directly.

You can keep perfecting the old pitch while the customer moves on without you. The customer doesn't owe your business model another decade.

Watch what's changing. Payment habits. Distribution. Costs. What people value. Where they're already spending. Ask who can help solve the problem without building every part yourself.

A promotion can bring customers back. A partner can provide something you can't. A useful introduction can become an income stream. Find the connection.

Don't just sit in the mess and complain that things used to be easier.

Get this in your head.

Find the new problem. Get useful. Get paid.

18

Staying power  ·  2 min read

Shut up. Listen. Then do something.

You only learn by listening.

Most people, once they start a business, won't shut the fuck up. About their business.

How smart they are. What they turn over. How they're doing everything. That is not going to teach you anything.

Anyone who's making money one way or another knows something you don't know. There's a lesson everywhere.

Look at the business in front of you.

You're in a coffee shop. How many customers come in? What do they spend? What else do they buy? What's the rent? How many staff? Where's the money actually made?

You don't know the rent. Fine. Make an estimate and remember it's an estimate. Notice the queue. The seats. The sign. The booking process that makes you want to give up. Stop walking past the lesson every day.

The first time is supposed to be harder.

First time you make a website, you have to go out there, you have to source all these different companies, you have to find a member of staff to make it, and you make it, and you make mistakes, and you need to change it because the SEO ain't set up, blah, blah, whatever.

By the third or fourth time, you've got people, methods, and mistakes you don't need to repeat. Keep what you learn. Use it again.

Aim higher. But sell something first.

Be realistic. Don't be a dick. But you do need to aim too high.

Don't brag about a half-million turnover before you've sold one thing. Get the engine working. Then ask how much further you can take it.

Money comes in on Tuesday. What do you do Wednesday?

I wake up and I try and find some more fucking money.

Work. Deliver. Recover. Come back and do it again. Reading about action is still reading. Go and get something done.

Get this in your head.

You’ve read it. What are you going to do with it?
