Part I

Start the engine

Speed, first deals, cheap starts, people, and finding the money.

01

Start the engine  ·  3 min read

Move fast. Keep the quality.

I want to talk about business: making an offer, getting it in front of people, and getting paid. The first thing I want you to understand is speed.

I am not going to tell you that a positive thought puts money in your bank. You can read books all day. You still have to go out and do something. Knowledge matters when you use it. I have worked jobs, sold advertising, run companies, made money and made mistakes. What I want to give you is the way I approach those situations, beginning with the thing I come back to over and over: speed.

Speed is extremely important in business. Everything that needs to be done must be done fast.

Now, do not mix that up with doing a bad job. People seem to conflate the two. That if you do something quickly, it must be terrible. That's not the case. When I train to fight, my instructor tells me to punch faster. He is not asking me to throw the technique away. He wants the same movement, performed more quickly.

You keep the same quality of punch, but you do it quicker.

I use a plane as the picture in my head. It has momentum; it does not instantly drop when it has a problem. That is a metaphor for keeping things moving. I want work going out, people responding, and the next useful action happening. Momentum gives me options. It does not make a broken business magically safe.

Say I need a website. Somebody tells me two weeks. I ask why. Can the useful version be done in three days? What actually needs those extra days? There is a difference between a real technical constraint and a comfortable estimate somebody has repeated for years. I want you to question that estimate, agree the scope, and find the person who can deliver it.

If I launch today, I can start hearing from buyers today. If I wait a week, that opportunity to learn is gone. The same applies once the business exists. Produce the advert, put it out, find out what happens. If I can produce two useful adverts in the time somebody else produces one, I get another chance to improve the result.

“I have a business idea.”
“Okay, cool.”
A week later: “How is your business?”
“What business?”

That is the gap I am talking about. An idea has been sitting there for a week and nothing has moved. Make the next action smaller if you need to. Then do it. Stop using a vague future date to avoid a clear job today.

Get this in your head.

Keep the standard. Cut the waiting.

02

Start the engine  ·  3 min read

I sold the deals. The margin was missing.

At twenty, I learned how much a determined salesperson could bring in. Then I learned that a big turnover could hide a very weak business.

I applied for a job selling television advertising. They wanted a marketing degree. I wanted real experience and money in my pocket. I offered to work for a week so they could judge the work. They gave me the job. I was twenty, and I got on the phone.

I called everyone. People in the office laughed when I called very large companies because we were a small operation. I called anyway. I would open a newspaper, look at the adverts and work through them. If a company was already paying to advertise, I knew there was an advertising budget and somebody responsible for spending it.

Three deals. Forty thousand each. One hundred and twenty thousand in sales.

I earned ten percent commission: twelve thousand for me. I had never had that kind of money. Soon I was having lunch with the experienced top salesperson, and he wanted to start an agency with me as managing director. I had only been there about a month. By a month and a half, I had left and become a fifty-percent owner of a television advertising business.

My partner had a drug problem. In practice, I ended up running the company. We sold very cheap packages: about £18,000 or £19,000 for the commercial, the work needed to get it approved, and around 150 television spots. Eventually we had about ten people calling prospects. We had plenty of clients to show in meetings, so people thought we were brilliant young marketers.

Here was the sh*t underneath it. On a package, we might have two or three thousand left before the rest of the business cost money. Some deals made nothing. Some lost money. We had cars, an office and wages, but the package prices did not support all of that.

How did we pay to finish an old deal? We landed a new one. Then another. At roughly two deals a week, the first year's turnover approached a million pounds. There was money moving, but there was not the profit the outside picture suggested.

I kept that going for around a year and a half. Then I went away for a month to train. My partner emptied the bank account, new deals did not come in, and everything broke at once. We lost the office. Creditors came to the door. I could not sleep.

I learned why speed matters, and I also learned what speed cannot fix. Selling faster kept the doors open. It did not turn an underpriced deal into a healthy one. I needed control of the cash, a workable margin and an operation that did not fall apart when I was away.

Get this in your head.

Incoming cash buys time. Positive economics make that time useful.

03

Start the engine  ·  3 min read

Where is the money coming from?

When somebody explains a new company to me, I do not start with the logo. I start with who is going to pay.

A business is money in.

That is the question I want at the front of your mind. I hear people talk about their accountant, trademark, website, office and equipment. Fine. Where is the person handing over money? What are they buying? Why do they want it? How are they going to pay you?

Imagine I ask you how to start a makeup company. You tell me you need makeup, a logo, labels, stock, a warehouse, a website and advertising. Listen to the list. You are explaining how to spend money. I am asking how we make it.

I am not impressed by how much a founder has bought. Anybody can spend. Buying the visible pieces gives you the feeling that you have started. You can tell your friends you own a company. But if nobody wants the offer, the expensive pieces do not rescue you.

Before the office, the extra laptop and the boxes around your bed, find the buyer.

With the makeup example, I would want a clear offer, prices and a way to see whether people actually want the product. Put the proposition in front of them. Do they respond? Do they want to order? If the answer is no, I want to learn that before I have five hundred boxes sitting in my bedroom.

And here is the boundary: I cannot call a product available when it is not, then invent a story about demand to explain the delay. A paid pre-order needs to be a paid pre-order. Tell the customer what exists, what is still being arranged and when delivery is expected. A gift does not replace a promise I can keep.

The order I care about is simple. I identify the customer. I explain the offer. I make the price clear. I give that person a sensible way to pay. I know how I will deliver. Then I see if the offer works. The exact payment method is secondary to having that route properly thought through.

If I spend heavily on everything around the sale and only then discover nobody wants it, I have bought myself a very expensive answer. If I run a small, honest demand test first, I can change direction without carrying a warehouse of unwanted stock.

When you want to start a business, your number one concern is how do I get money in?

Get that question right. Fulfillment still matters. Your commitments still matter. But stop letting the comfortable work of buying things hide the uncomfortable work of finding someone willing to buy from you.

Get this in your head.

Write the payment path before the shopping list.

04

Start the engine  ·  3 min read

Start small. Buy more chances.

I want a new idea to earn the next investment. I do not want to spend the whole budget proving that my first guess was wrong.

You can start businesses very, very cheaply if you do it the right way.

People come to me asking for fifty thousand, a hundred thousand, two hundred thousand to begin. Sometimes a project genuinely needs capital. If you are building aircraft, obviously the equipment is not free. But do not take the most expensive possible version of a small business and decide that is your only way in.

Go back to the makeup example. Put all the setup together: office, stock, staff, trademarks, accounts, advertising. Say that version needs 200,000. Now imagine a narrow launch that tests demand and costs 5,000. I am using those as example figures. That is a difference of 195,000 before I know whether the offer works.

If both ideas fail, the money at risk is very different. One failure takes five thousand. The other takes two hundred thousand. For the same initial sum, I can run forty experiments at five thousand each. It does not guarantee one succeeds. It does mean one bad guess has not used up all my room to move.

I want the market to earn the bigger budget.

If money begins coming in and I can deliver properly, I can reinvest some of the proceeds. I can improve the next version using evidence from actual buyers. That is a different conversation from funding a perfect-looking operation before the first customer exists.

When I landed the first deal for my advertising agency, I had a phone, a laptop, a business card and a bank account. I went to the client's office. I signed the deal. The customer paid, and that money helped set up the operation. I did not need to impress the prospect with my own expensive office first.

I still had the twelve thousand from my earlier commission, but I kept that aside in case things went wrong. I could have spent it on desks and computers. Instead, I used what I already had and tried to get the company funded by the work it was winning.

When you're broke, it's hard to make mistakes, because you don't have any money to lose. When you're rich, it's easy to make mistakes, because you have a lot of money to lose. That is why I still question a launch budget even when I can afford it. A smaller beginning forces me to separate what makes a sale possible from what merely makes me feel established.

Get this in your head.

Risk a smaller amount to learn whether the idea deserves more.

05

Start the engine  ·  3 min read

Build the catalogue before the big office.

Give me a completely different business and I still start with the same questions: how do we make money, what can we already deliver, and how fast can we find out?

Say you want to start a record label. I have never run one. I am working this out from the basic principles in front of you. How does it make money? It sells music. So, before I spend money on a fancy studio or an office, I need music I can actually sell.

With a digital product, I need the finished material. I cannot distribute songs that do not exist. I would look for independent artists who want help reaching an audience and would consider a revenue-sharing agreement instead of a large payment up front.

Say a recording sells for £1 and we agree a fifty-fifty split. The artist gets half; the label gets half before its own costs. Now I have something to work with. The next job is distribution: where are the listeners, and how do I put this music in front of them?

You have to test the distribution channels. Test them out. Test out an independent distribution channel.

I would use the usual music outlets and look at direct channels too. A website. Performance videos. A video channel. Somewhere the band can tell its story and show what it is doing. I am not paying for a room full of staff before I know which route reaches a buyer.

I would ask the artists for more useful material: songs, recordings of live performances, videos from rehearsals. Maybe a daily video diary while they record. Keep producing. Keep publishing. Keep finding out what people respond to. A catalogue gives me more than one chance at reaching an audience.

“What have you actually done for the artists?”
“Here is the music. Here are the performances. Here is where we distribute it. Here is the audience we have helped them reach.”

When I approach a stronger artist later, I can show that work. I am no longer explaining an imaginary company. I have a catalogue and evidence of promotion. The deal might be different: perhaps the artist receives sixty percent and I receive forty. That can still work if the partnership creates more value.

I do not need to pretend I can guarantee a hundred thousand sales a month. That is b*llsh*t if I cannot back it up. Be clear about what I will do, what the artist will provide and how the money is shared. If the music does not sell, investigate the music, audience and distribution together. Do not hide behind a promise I invented.

Notice what I have done. The first thing I wrote is how do we make the money? Then I found a product I could deliver without buying a big operation. Then I worked on getting it out quickly. If the experiment struggles after six months, I have learned something without leaving a large office and equipment bill behind me.

Get this in your head.

Start with a deliverable offer, share the upside, and prove the route to buyers.

06

Start the engine  ·  4 min read

Find your people. Pool what you can.

Some of the people you need may already be around you. Look for real ability and shared commitment before you start buying an expensive organization.

I hear the rule all the time: do not mix friends or family with business. I do not accept that as a rule for everybody. My brother works with me. My cousin works with me. The question is whether the person is capable, whether we can rely on one another, and whether we both understand the deal.

A younger person in your circle may know more about computers, editing or new media than you do. I am thirty-three in this story, and I have had eighteen- and nineteen-year-olds point out things I did not know. Good. I do not need to know every tool. I need to recognize what they can do and give the work a clear direction.

Imagine a small website service. The customer pays $300: half now, half on completion. The person building the site gets $50 at the start and $50 when it is done. That leaves $200 before the other expenses of finding the customer and managing the job. It is a simple illustration of selling, organizing and delivering as different roles.

The terms have to make sense to everyone. I am not treating a child or an unpaid relative as free equipment. If I discuss something like ten percent ownership, it needs to mean something clear. People need to understand their work, their pay, their risk and their share of any upside.

My brother and I also learned the value of sharing resources from a family we knew. One brother was a junior lawyer making perhaps £50,000 a year, yet he had access to a very expensive car. We asked how. He had six brothers, working in different professions. They shared a house, pooled resources and shared the vehicles.

The car payment in that example was around £2,000 a month. One person did not use the car all the time. The family did not need six copies of everything. The point that stayed with us was not the car. It was the money going out separately that did not need to go out separately.

We rented together rather than paying for two apartments. We put resources into the same pot. Our first used performance car cost £65,000, and we bought it together. The money we did not spend duplicating our lives could support what we were building.

Later, my cousin joined us. I had first met him when he was five and saw him again at nineteen. He came from a comfortable background, then went through a difficult stretch, sleeping on a floor and delivering pizzas. After a couple of years of that struggle, he became part of our team.

You may not have a brother or a cousin. Fine. Think about the people you trust and the people who trust you. Those are the people you want to work with. Build the agreement around real cooperation, not around a surname. Pooling only helps when the people in the pool are pulling in the same direction.

Run the numbers yourself. Keep the delivery cost in view while you move the price.

Get this in your head.

Share resources when the trust, expectations and benefits are shared too.

07

Start the engine  ·  3 min read

Make your word carry weight.

When I stood in front of a client, the words were only part of the meeting. How I carried myself mattered to the conversation too.

Command respect.

That is the next point. I have been a professional fighter, and physical training is a large part of my life. In sales meetings, I felt that the discipline and confidence I brought into the room helped people take me seriously. I was not disappearing behind the desk, mumbling a pitch and hoping nobody asked a question.

My experienced sales partner had presence too. I learned a lot by watching how he spoke, how he held the conversation and how comfortable he was making a direct offer. A person can feel strong in the room before the numbers are even discussed.

There's some things that are spoken without being spoken. People form impressions. They look for signals that I take myself seriously, that I can handle difficult work and that I believe what I am saying. Preparation, clear speech and deliberate presentation all affect that.

But I am not asking you to confuse appearance with competence. A physique is not proof that an advert will produce a return. If I stand up and promise a client twice their money back on a nineteen-thousand package, I still need something real behind that promise. A confident unsupported guarantee is still unsupported.

Small differences in the result can matter enormously. Take $1 million in sales. At a twenty-percent margin, that is $200,000. At a thirty-percent margin, it is $300,000. That is another $100,000. Be precise: the margin increased by ten percentage points. That is not the same as profit increasing by ten percent.

That calculation shows why I pay attention to details that affect the deal. It does not prove that physical appearance creates the change. I need to find which details actually make the offer stronger, make the conversation clearer and make the customer more comfortable buying.

I used the same basic approach in very different operations. My adult webcam business began with equipment I already had and an adult partner who wanted to work with me. I was still thinking about demand, low costs, speed and getting the work produced. Presence did not replace any of those things.

Do not hide behind a weak presentation. Do not use a strong presentation to hide weak work.

Get yourself ready for the room. Speak clearly. Know the offer. Then make your word worth something by keeping it.

Get this in your head.

Present yourself deliberately, then back the presentation with delivery.

08

Start the engine  ·  3 min read

Sell to the people who already trust you.

Before I pay to find a whole new audience, I look at the people who have already bought from me. What do they need next?

One of the easiest ways I found to bring in money was to go back to a customer who had already bought and received something they liked. The first sale introduces us. Good delivery gives the second conversation a different starting point.

At the advertising agency, the first package might be eighteen or nineteen thousand. We produced the advert and showed it to the client. If they liked it, then we offered an additional airtime package.

We didn't, we never tried to sell it to them before they saw the advert because then it was much harder to sell.

The timing mattered. Before the advert existed, it was another promise. After they had watched something they were happy with, they could picture it being broadcast more often. The product was real to them.

The extra package was around £5,000, and I had about £4,000 of profit in that additional package. We could not easily sell the whole thing as a twenty-four- or twenty-five-thousand bundle at the start. Selling the original deal and offering the extra later worked better for us. When the client liked the advert, I was seeing around sixty to seventy percent take the add-on.

I am talking about what happened in that business, not giving you a conversion rate to assume in yours. The important part is the sequence: deliver, establish confidence, then show the extra value. Be honest about the airtime, the placement and what the buyer is getting. A made-up bulk-buy story does not create genuine value.

A business owner once asked me for two hundred thousand because he was short of cash. I asked how many previous customers he had. Around ten thousand in the database. I told him to look there before looking at me for a loan. Put together a relevant offer. Contact the people who already know the business and have had a good experience.

Your next sale may be in the customer list you already have.

I would ask that question before spending more on ads or building an entirely new product. Can the customer reorder? Do they need a sensible upgrade? Is there an existing service they have not used? The offer still has to suit them, and the numbers still have to work.

In business, you don't need to sell to tons of people if you can sell to the same people over and over again.

Get this in your head.

Deliver something useful, then make a useful next offer.

09

Start the engine  ·  4 min read

Use what you have before buying what you lack.

I do not want the feeling of owning a company to become a substitute for actually earning. Look around before you ask somebody else to fund the whole thing.

I know people who have the registration, accountant and folders ready before anybody has bought anything. It feels productive. It gives them a company to talk about. But the customer question is still unanswered.

I have started perhaps a hundred companies in my life. Around twenty made money. Think how much time and money I could bury in elaborate setups for ideas that never get anywhere. I do not want optional complexity to become the first job of every experiment.

That does not mean ignoring tax, customer promises or whatever requirements apply to the work. Do what is required. Stop buying extra ceremony because selling makes you uncomfortable. A company that exists on paper still needs a reason for somebody to pay it.

Now take a piece of paper and write down what you already have. A laptop. An internet connection. A place to work. A car or access to transport. Physical strength. A useful skill. Someone you know who can design, edit, pack orders or help with delivery.

When I was thinking about an adult-entertainment business, I first thought of a physical club. At the time I had six girlfriends, and that was part of what prompted the idea. But a personal relationship is not a work agreement; any commercial role needs willing adults and clear terms. Then I looked at the building, fit-out and operating costs. I could be risking three or four hundred thousand before money came in. Too much.

I asked a different question: what is the customer actually paying for, and can that demand be served without all the costs of a physical venue? That took me to online webcam services. I already had the equipment and connection, and an adult partner willing to try it.

The day I had the webcam idea, the same day I was making money.

That is what I mean by using what you have. I did not begin by buying a large operation. I began with an available setup. When that generated money, I could buy another laptop. Then add capacity again. One setup became two. Then a bigger apartment, then more people and more equipment.

I never spent money I didn't make first.

That operation went on to generate over $7 million. My point is the order: earn, then expand. I was not repeatedly reaching into my own pocket to build a picture of a business before finding out whether anyone wanted it.

The same question works with ordinary services. Maybe someone in the family wants to help dispatch parcels. Maybe I can find lawn-care customers and work with someone who does the gardening. Maybe access to a car and a capable driver suggests a transport service. The people need fair terms, and the work has to be properly set up. I am looking for a useful arrangement, not an excuse to deceive them.

I do not accept “I need an investor” as the automatic first answer. Show me the smaller version. Show me what you already control. Show me the first thing you can deliver. Sometimes the constraint is not a missing resource. It is that you have not noticed the resources around you.

Get this in your head.

List the tools, skills, access and willing people already available to you.

10

Start the engine  ·  4 min read

Give every role a clear job.

I do not hire an assistant because I like saying I have an assistant. I want to know what the role changes in the business.

Staff need a clear objective.

Here is how I start thinking about a role: does it help bring money in, or does it free useful time? A salesperson has an obvious connection to revenue. An assistant may save hours that I can put into work only I can do. Delivery, support and operations can make both of those possible. But I still need a clear connection to value.

“I need an assistant.”
“An assistant to do what?”
“Just to help with things.”
“What things?”

I keep asking. Which tasks? How long do they take? What does good completion look like? What will I do with the capacity that comes back to me? If I cannot describe the work, I am not ready to describe the job.

Get the paper out periodically. I might review it every couple of weeks or once a month. What contribution did a role create? What did it cost? What tasks were completed? How long would those tasks have taken the rest of the team? I want the answer on the page rather than a vague feeling that everybody looks busy.

I have been on the other side. After the first agency failed, I worked selling online advertising and websites. I was training hard and tired. Reputation is tied into it too. I knew some people mattered to future trading relationships, and I thought about whether a dispute would travel. But a distant supplier is still a person on the other side of an agreement. Distance does not turn the relationship into nothing.

I do not want the company to be rich on paper while I am broke. I also do not want to call the owner rich because obligations have been pushed out of sight. Work out the true position. That tells me much more than staring at the biggest number in the account.

Finally, I come back to the mood of the team. In the little office, my positive energy mattered. When I walked in, spoke about the next deal, and made people feel the work was moving, the room changed. When I left, that energy vanished.

I can call that my people-management lesson: the leader's behaviour spreads. But a good mood cannot replace clear expectations or money I owe. Positivity can support a working team. It cannot carry the entire arrangement on its own. The ten-day absence had already shown me what happens when the arrangement is too thin underneath the enthusiasm.

Get this in your head.

Know what belongs to fulfilment, what is owed, and what can really support the owner.

64

Make the whole thing work  ·  6 min read

Change the presentation. Match the person. Explain the need now.

I finish this stretch by returning to the way an offer is presented. The same item can mean different things to different people, and a distant benefit may not answer the problem on their mind today.

People ask me what they should sell. I ask how they could sell. What reach do they have? What do people already come to them for? Which useful product could fit that route? The object matters, but the way it reaches the buyer and the meaning around it can matter too.

I pick up a pen as an example. I could talk about the plastic, the colour, and the ink. Or I could put a story around it: important contracts, ideas, and the work a person hopes to do with it. I used a price of $38. I was showing how an ordinary object can be given a very different place in the buyer's mind.

Play to people's strengths.

A story is not a magic feature. If I promise the pen will create million-dollar ideas, I have moved from explaining meaning to inventing a result. I do not need that claim to see the difference between a bare object and a relevant presentation.

In the studio, I had a performer who thought she had run out of things to sell. Videos had not worked. Other offers had not worked. I watched the questions people were asking and changed the setting and the story around the performance.

I moved her from the bedroom to the bathroom. She whispered, and we played out an invented risk of being interrupted. I even described knocking on the door as part of it. The audience reacted strongly, and she kept that setup for the next two months.

I didn't try and change the what, I changed the how.

I had been trying to find a new thing to sell. In the end, changing the way I presented the same thing was enough. I watched what people responded to, changed the setting, and kept the setup that worked.

Next I look at the people doing the work. A knight and a bishop do not move the same way on a chessboard. I win by understanding what each piece can do, rather than ordering both to behave identically. The same thought applies to a team.

Some people handle pressure well. Some are good at adapting. Some can explain. Some can make the thing look right. I am not particularly good with cameras and editing, so I hire creative people who can do those parts properly. Knowing what I want is not the same as knowing how to produce every frame.

I should know my own limits as clearly as I know my strengths. The job is to put the right ability on the right problem. Details matter, but relevant ability matters more than deciding what somebody should do from their appearance.

The buyer is different each time too. In the agency, if a company was already selling plenty, I might talk about profile, positioning, and what a campaign could mean in its category. If another company desperately wanted more sales, I would not give it the same long speech about prestige. I would talk about reaching customers and what we could realistically do for that goal.

I used a simple question: are you selling enough? Usually the answer was that they would like to sell more. Now I knew something about what they wanted. That was the opening. It did not prove television would solve every problem, but it told me which problem the buyer wanted to discuss.

The pen returns here as another reminder. A person staring at a blank page may care more about finding something worth writing than about how many months the ink lasts. I need to understand that desire, then be honest about how my offer relates to it. Confidence and curiosity can help someone look. They cannot create powers the product does not have.

Finally, I was discussing a back-end system with someone in one of my businesses. The conversation kept going towards what might happen in two years. I wanted to know what problem we were solving now. People have yesterday's trouble and tomorrow's bills on their minds. A distant possibility can feel very small beside those things.

Immediate, not later.

With television advertising, a prospect might say they were thinking about branding next year. I tried to bring the conversation back to a current reason to act. Timing can matter. But I need an actual reason, rather than simply making the buyer frightened that somebody else is ahead.

Future benefits can support the offer. I want the present benefit to be clear enough to lead it. What changes for the buyer now? What current cost, inconvenience, or goal does this address?

I never worked in car sales, although I thought I would be good at it. When I buy a car, I recognise the conversation. I say the car I have is fine and I might replace it next year. The salesperson points to what a change could mean now: fuel costs, tax, insurance, and the driving assistance on the new car. They are trying to turn a future purchase into a current decision by making today's value more concrete.

Get this in your head.

Use the right strengths, understand the buyer’s real goal, and make the present value concrete.
